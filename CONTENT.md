Purpose: a working content system for his fractional/interim VP of
Product positioning — a backlog of specific posts to write, the
higher-level topic categories that generate more of them over time, a
watchlist of people posting in this space, a ranked library of their
best posts for inspiration, and the best-practice guidance behind it
all. Complements `CONTRACTING.md` (which covers the *strategy* of
marketing himself as a fractional exec, section 2) — this file is the
day-to-day *content* engine that feeds off that strategy.

## How to use this file
- Section 1 is the actionable backlog — pull from here when it's time to
  write a post.
- Section 2 is the idea generator — when the 10-post backlog runs low,
  come back here and derive new specific posts from these categories.
- Sections 3-4 are for inspiration when stuck — who to look at, and
  their actual best posts, ranked.
- Section 5 defines how this file stays current — see that section for
  the weekly-check mechanism and its current status.
- Section 6 is for expanding beyond LinkedIn once that channel is
  proven.
- Section 7 is the reference to check tone/approach against before
  publishing anything.

**Full text of #11 (locked 2026-07-27, posted 2026-07-28):**

> If you're in product right now — at any level — there's a good chance the last year has felt relentless.
>
> If you're a PM or senior PM: layoffs, reorgs, roadmaps rewritten overnight around a new AI initiative. A "final" round of layoffs that turns out not to be final.
>
> If you're a VP of Product or CPO, the storm looks different but hits harder in its own way. You're the one who has to lead the change while absorbing it. Rebuild how your PMs work. Roll out AI across the org — with no playbook, because nobody has one yet. Answer to a board that wants an AI story this quarter, while still delivering everything you committed to before AI changed the plan. And do all of it while your team watches how you react, because your steadiness is the only stability they have left.
>
> Different seats, same storm.
>
> Here's what I keep hearing, from ICs and CPOs alike: "I thought I was the only one struggling with this." Over the past year, I've been close to a lot of these transitions — product teams reorganizing, adopting AI in how they build and what they ship, and finding their footing on the other side. And what that experience tells me, with confidence: almost everyone, at every level, is going through some version of this. Nobody has it fully figured out. The ones who look like they do are just further along the same messy path.
>
> Once you stop seeing this as something happening to you and start seeing it as a transition the entire industry is making, it becomes something more manageable: a problem to solve. And if you're in product, solving problems is the job.
>
> It's also a genuine window. The PMs who rebuilt their workflow around AI this year are shipping work that used to take a small team. The product leaders who used the disruption to redesign their org — instead of just defending the old one — are building trust that will outlast the chaos: with their teams, with their own leadership, and with themselves.
>
> One more thing worth saying: AI can accelerate your work, but it can't tell you when you're fooling yourself. Only people who've actually been through this can do that. So don't figure it out alone — talk to other product people, compare notes. It helps more than you'd expect.
>
> If you've come through a reorg, an AI transition, or a wave of change you didn't choose — and found something that worked — share it below. One practical thing that helped. Someone reading this needs it more than you know.
>
> And if you're in the middle of it right now, feel free to reach out — sometimes it just helps to talk it through with someone who's seen it before.

**Research backing for #12 (2026-08-04) — 5 hypotheses on how AI is changing PM/CPO/VP-of-Product roles, 5 sources each:**

**Full text of #12 (drafted and posted 2026-08-04):**

> Pick one: "AI is quietly eliminating the product manager job," or "companies can't find enough senior product talent and are paying record premiums for it."
>
> Both are true. At the same time. And once you see why, you'll know exactly which side of the split you want to be standing on.
>
> If you're a PM or senior PM, you've probably felt the first half already. AI now writes the first draft of the PRD, synthesizes the customer feedback, builds the competitive analysis. The tasks that used to fill a junior PM's week are the same ones AI now does before lunch. Some research puts product manager headcount down significantly this year, and the drop is concentrated almost entirely in that first layer of the job — the "creation" work, not the "decide what matters" work.
>
> Here's the part that doesn't fit the doom headline, though. At the exact same time, a distinct "AI product manager" specialization has emerged — and it's commanding a real pay premium over the generalist role, because the ability to bridge AI capability, data, and product judgment is suddenly in short supply. Some companies are going further and merging product and engineering into a single hybrid operator who can specify, design, and ship with AI tools alone. LinkedIn didn't shrink its associate product manager program this year. It rebuilt it into exactly this hybrid.
>
> So the job isn't disappearing. It's splitting. The execution layer is shrinking fast. The judgment layer is growing, and getting paid more to do it.
>
> If you're a VP of Product or CPO, you're watching the same split from one level up, and it looks a little different from there. Organizations aren't hunting for the most technically fluent AI person in the room anymore. They want the person who can bridge technology and business, drive AI adoption across an entire org — not just one product line — and get a skeptical board to actually believe the plan. Translator, not technician.
>
> But the less obvious half of this story is what's happening on the candidate side. I've seen executive search data, built from hundreds of real senior AI/product leadership searches, that says something pretty blunt: competitive compensation cannot offset unclear authority or weak organizational support. The strongest candidates are increasingly walking away from roles with impressive titles and thin mandates. They're filtering for scope of impact and real executive sponsorship before they're filtering for the number on the offer letter.
>
> Put those two halves together and you get a matching market, not just a hiring trend. The PMs building AI-native judgment are getting pickier about which companies deserve it. The leaders being recruited to run AI transformation are getting pickier about who actually hands them the authority to do it. Everyone serious, on both sides of the table, is now filtering for the same thing: real mandate, not just a title.
>
> Where are you feeling this split right now — in your own team, or in your own job search? Curious what you're seeing.

  been run.

  role itself is/isn't. Maps to #10. *Future prompts:* what "operator
  judgment" actually means in practice; the DevOps/AI-automation habits

  stat. Found to reliably *underperform* unless paired with a personal
  story or specific claim (Maxfield's stat-only post got only 7
  reactions) — never post a stat alone; always attach his own take or a
  concrete example.

## 7. Best practices — do/don't, tone, examples
*Consolidated from CONTRACTING.md §2's research (full sourcing there)
plus the ranked library in §4 above, which doubles as good/bad examples.*

**Do:**
- Post specific dialogue/story or an original framework — the two
  formats that dominate the top of §4's ranking.
- Quantify everything — case studies need real before/after numbers,
  not "significant improvement."
- Write something genuinely contestable when doing a debate-style post
  — safe consensus statements draw far fewer comments (compare rank 1-2
  vs. rank 8 in §4).
- Keep posting cadence to 3-5x/week including ~1 video/week once a
  rhythm is established (see CONTRACTING.md §2) — not required from
  day one.
- Tone: first-person, direct, willing to state a real opinion — every
  high performer in §4 has a clear point of view, not a hedge.

**Don't:**
- Don't post a generic pros/cons checklist — this is the *lowest*-
  performing format found (§4, rank 12).
- Don't reshare someone else's guide expecting their engagement to
  transfer — it doesn't (§4, rank 10 vs. rank 4, same underlying guide).
- Don't lead with a market stat alone — pair it with a personal story or
  specific claim, or skip it (§4, rank 11).
- Don't make availability/"open to work" the subject of a post — keep
  that in the profile, not the content (CONTRACTING.md §2).

**Good examples to study (see §4 for links):** rank 1 (Mackey — story),
rank 2 (Gupta — framework), rank 3 (Schneider — stage-fit explainer).

**Weaker examples to learn from, not copy (see §4 for links):** rank
10-12 (Hensley x2, Maxfield) — all three are generically-formatted or
un-personalized despite covering reasonable topics, which is precisely
why format/specificity matters more than topic choice alone.

he gains real posting experience — e.g. what actually worked for his
own audience vs. what the research predicted.*
